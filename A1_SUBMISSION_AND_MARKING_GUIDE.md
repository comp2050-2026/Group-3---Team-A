# A1 Problem Analysis — Submission and Marking Guide

> [!important] Specification authority
> This note records the supplied A1 guidance in an AI-readable form. The released iLearn A1 specification and submission box control any later changes.

## What the assignment is testing

A1 is about requirements elicitation, requirements analysis and requirements specification. It is not a design assignment. Teams should investigate a non-trivial problem, engage genuinely with stakeholders and document what the proposed system needs to do and why.

The assessment deliberately avoids a conventional prescriptive rubric. Students are expected to read the situation, make professional judgements and demonstrate thoughtful, creative and defensible requirements work while still following standard SRS formats.

## What to submit

The team submits two files.

### 1. One PDF — 80 marks

The PDF contains, in this order:

1. The SRS based on the repository's [SRS skeleton](a1docs/SRS.md).
2. The Group Activity Record defined by that skeleton.
3. Appendices and supporting evidence defined by that skeleton.

### 2. One handover explainer video — 20 marks

- Duration: 5–8 minutes.
- Audience: the design team that will receive the SRS and continue the project in A2.
- Explain the system's key required features.
- Explain the main actors from the use-case diagram and important interactions discovered through client consultation.
- Select one interaction diagram and explain its steps and the reasoning behind that interaction.
- Divide the presentation so every team member contributes visibly and is identifiable.
- Upload a video file to iLearn, not a link to an external host.
- A Zoom recording is acceptable. Keep the iLearn upload limit in mind; the supplied guidance estimates roughly 150–200 MB.

## How to approach the work

- Distinguish elicitation from specification and demonstrate both.
- Use stakeholder discussions intentionally: plan questions, record answers, identify gaps and follow up.
- Develop a suitable vision statement, overall description, requirements, fit criteria, use cases and interaction diagrams.
- Keep the work in analysis/requirements scope; do not drift into solution design.
- Maintain meeting minutes, third-party-resource records and repository evidence.
- Record who was assigned each task, what was agreed, progress updates, delays, illness or uncertainty, and how the team responded.
- Use GitHub as a collaboration and evidence system, not merely as a final upload location.

## The two audiences

### Teaching team

The teaching team looks for evidence that students understand SRS structure, use cases, requirements and elicitation, and that the team engaged seriously with the problem.

### Receiving design team and stakeholders

The SRS must be usable by people who continue the work. They need sufficient, relevant and unambiguous detail to design a system. Teams should seek stakeholder feedback and assess their own SRS from the receiving team's perspective.

## Marking model

A1 is marked out of 100 and contributes 30% of the unit grade.

### PDF — 80 marks

| Component | Marks |
| --- | ---: |
| SRS introduction, overall presentation and professionalism | 10 |
| SRS overall description | 10 |
| SRS specific requirements | 10 |
| Use-case diagram | 10 |
| Three use-case descriptions | 10 |
| Three interaction diagrams consistent with those use cases | 10 |
| Group Activity Record | 10 |
| Appendices | 10 |
| **PDF total** | **80** |

### Video — 20 marks

| Component | Marks |
| --- | ---: |
| Professionalism and suitability for the handover purpose | 10 |
| Content and explanation of the SRS's core ideas | 10 |
| **Video total** | **20** |

## Qualitative marking guidance

### Professionalism and completeness

- Clear, professional writing and structure.
- Legible, high-quality figures and diagrams.
- Current, relevant references.
- Complete team information and submission artefacts.
- Careful layout and presentation.
- Evidence of engagement and thinking beyond minimum compliance.

### Introduction and overall description

- A useful introduction and project description for the intended audience.
- A clear understanding of the problem, scope, users, constraints and context.

### Requirements and use cases

- Realistic, feasible and carefully reasoned requirements.
- Meaningful stakeholder engagement.
- Consistent use cases, descriptions and interaction diagrams.
- Requirements with suitable measurable/fit criteria where required by the template.

### Discussion

- Serious analysis of elicitation techniques and real interactions.
- Conclusions supported by evidence.
- Constructive recommendations and explicit unresolved issues.

### Teamwork and individualisation

- Evidence of continuous collaboration rather than isolated pieces merged at the deadline.
- Shared responsibility rather than one person doing the work.
- Genuine stakeholder communication and meaningful GitHub activity.
- Group Activity Record and appendices provide checks on allocations, agreements, progress and communication.
- The group deliverable receives feedback, but reported individual contributions and the iLearn group-feedback tool are used to individualise marks.

## Quality questions before submission

- Does the team understand how the proposed system would work?
- Has it investigated difficulties and ambiguities rather than hiding them?
- Are decisions creative, feasible and explained clearly?
- Can the receiving team continue from the SRS without guessing critical details?
- Are unresolved issues documented after reasonable attempts to investigate them?
- Does the submission meet both formal-documentation standards and the needs of a real-world stakeholder/development context?
